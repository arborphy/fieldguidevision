A computer that names a tree from a leaf photo, and says why

From a leaf photo, the computer names the tree and says why, like a botanist: “toothed edge ✓, simple leaf ✓, alternate ✓ → American beech”.

Apps can already guess a plant name. But they do not tell you why, so you cannot check them. We teach the computer to read the same leaf features a field guide uses, and to compare them with the guide.

How it works: two YOLO levels between photo and field guide

1Photo
iNaturalist
A website where people share plant photos; other users check the names.
→
2Leaf-photo sorter
logistic regression on DINOv3 features
DINOv3 (a general vision model from Meta) turns a photo into a list of numbers. A logistic regression (a simple yes/no scorer), trained on 1,641 hand-labelled BioImages photos, keeps photos where leaves are the main subject.
→
3Level 1: leaf finder
YOLO26s detector, taught by Grounding DINO-base
YOLO is a fast object detector. A slow, large detector (the “teacher”) drew leaf boxes; the small YOLO (the “student”) was trained to draw the same boxes. It cuts out single leaves.
→
4Level 2: leaf-edge reader
YOLO26s-cls classifier on leaf crops
Looks at each cut-out leaf and says: smooth, toothed or lobed edge. Labels come from the field-guide table, so nobody labels photos by hand.
→
5Field-guide matching
Go Botany
An expert field guide: 546 woody species and their traits. Each species is scored by how well the traits read from the photo fit it.
→
6Answer
species + reasons
The name, plus ✓ or ✗ for each trait. If a trait conflicts, it says “please check”.

Where the boxes come from: Grounding DINO-base, an object detector you can ask in words (“a leaf.”), drew the training boxes. A person looked through the boxes on all 716 test photos and found no major problems; the boxes were not corrected one by one.

Where the trait labels come from: the field-guide table. If the guide says a species has toothed leaves, every photo of that species counts as “toothed”. Testing always uses species the models never saw in training.

Level 1: the small YOLO copies the teacher’s leaf boxes about 24× faster

On photos of species it never saw, the student reaches mAP50 = 0.875. mAP50 is, roughly, the share of the teacher’s boxes that the student also finds with a box overlapping it by at least half, averaged over confidence levels. The student needs 12 ms per photo; the teacher needs 287 ms.

Caveats

  • The reference is the teacher’s boxes, so this measures imitation, not truth.
  • The best training round (epoch) was chosen on these same test photos, so the score is slightly optimistic.
  • Both teacher and student sometimes add one box covering almost the whole photo.

How we debugged. On a small subset, training with and without random image changes (augmentation) both learned (training-crop accuracy 0.95–0.99 and 0.95–0.98), so random cropping was not the cause. The fix changed three settings at once: test crops kept out of the training folder; balanced photo sampling instead of copying crops; 3 instead of 5 crops per photo. Which one caused the failure is not yet isolated.

Fairness. The fixed YOLO run used 1,760 training photos (262 species); the whole-photo BioCLIP reference used 3,689. BioCLIP may also have seen these species during its pre-training. So the gap is not yet a fair comparison.

We looked by eye at the 16 crops below (a visual check of 16 examples, not a measurement). The errors come from three sources:

  1. The box is not a single leaf (a whole branch or plant, or a whole compound leaf): about 6 of 16.
  2. The field-guide label does not match what this leaf shows: about 4 of 16. Rhododendron vaseyi is listed as smooth, but its edge is wavy with fine hairs; Gleditsia leaflets look smooth in photos; one Ilex opaca leaf has no spines.
  3. Real misses on subtle teeth: about 6 of 16. Myrica gale has teeth only near the tip; holly spines are read as lobes.

Takeaway: better boxes (the planned hand-checked “pro” labels) and per-leaf labels would fix about half of these errors.

What the first version already showed

1. A field-guide table is enough to teach traits, and more species help

Labels came only from species names plus the table, and the reader worked on unseen species. Training on more species (47 → 368) improved all three traits.

2. It explains its answers

For 95% of test photos the system can write a reason chain: all reasons fit (76%), or one does not and it asks you to check (19%).

Next: better boxes, a clean diagnosis, a fair comparison

  • Hand-checked “pro” boxes for level 1; retraining is cheap (about 25 GPU minutes).
  • Find the cause of the first-run failure with three runs that each change one setting (about 3 minutes each).
  • Train level 2 on all photos and for more epochs, then compare YOLO and BioCLIP fairly on identical training data.
  • Leafsnap close-ups (single leaves on white) to teach fine leaf edges.
Appendix: method details
  • Scores. Trait scores are balanced accuracy: the share of photos read correctly, averaged over the trait’s values. Labels use only species with one recorded value. Models are logistic regressions on frozen image features (the big models are not changed). Test species are never in training (S4/S8). Leaf crops for the first version: Grounding DINO boxes on 1,024-px photos (S7). Two-level YOLO (S9/S10): Grounding DINO-base boxes on all photos (S9) train the YOLO26s detector; YOLO26s-cls is trained on the top-3 crops per photo with Go Botany leaf-edge labels; species in training and test never overlap. DINOv3 features were also tried for traits; BioCLIP was more stable, so it is shown. Leaf-photo sorter: DINOv3 classifier, threshold chosen on validation photos (S2).
  • How far can leaves alone go? (S0) Using the field guide only, with all traits read perfectly: with 4 core leaf traits, a median of 41 of the 546 species share the same traits, and the right species is in the top 5 for 35%; with all 10 leaf traits, 79%. So leaf reasons narrow the list a lot but rarely pin one species; that is why they are used as reasons next to a name.
  • Naming without reasons (S5). A general model already names these common species well: BioCLIP’s first guess is right for 93% of the 716 test photos. Our contribution is the reasons.
  • Evidence groups (S6). Supported 76%, conflict 19%, no evidence (the traits read do not separate the answer from other species) 5%.
